Abstract
Traditional vision models that tokenize images
into uniform patches often fail to capture the
structured, semantically rich nature of charts
and BI (Business Intelligence) reports. In this
work, we introduce Chart-Aware Transformers,
a novel architecture that models charts as sequences of structured elements—such as titles,
axes, legends, bars, and annotations—rather
than pixel-based patches. Each element is embedded with a combination of class, textual,
and layout features, enabling the model to encode semantic and spatial relationships critical
for downstream tasks like chart retrieval, question answering, and document understanding.
By treating charts as compositional units with
explicit structure, our approach yields semantically faithful and information-rich representations, outperforming patch-based and purely
textual baselines. We demonstrate that this
element-centric formulation is better aligned
with the nature of chart data, unlocking new capabilities for intelligent document understanding in enterprise and analytics workflows.

Introduction

Understanding charts and Business Intelligence
(BI) reports is a critical challenge in visual document analysis. Traditional Vision Transformer
(ViT)-based models rely on patch-level image tokenization, which often fails to capture the structured, semantic, and spatial relationships between
elements in a chart. These models lack an explicit
representation of chart components like axes, legends, bars, titles, and annotations — elements essential for interpreting the information encoded in
structured visualizations.
In this paper, we introduce Chart-Aware Transformers, a novel architecture that explicitly models
chart elements as discrete tokens. Each token is
enriched with textual, semantic, and layout-aware
embeddings to reflect its class (e.g., “bar”, “x-axis”,
“title”), content (e.g., OCR-extracted text), and spa-

To train and evaluate our system, we generate

Each chart is annotated in COCO-style JSON format, ensuring compatibility with common vision
datasets and workflows.
The extracted chart elements are converted into
fixed-length feature vectors combining class embeddings, OCR/text embeddings, and normalized
layout information. These vectors are projected
and fed into a Transformer encoder, enabling downstream tasks like chart retrieval, semantic similarity,
and question answering.
This element-centric representation aligns with
the inherent structure of charts and offers several
advantages:
- It preserves semantic and spatial relationships
lost in patch-based models. - It enables reasoning
over individual elements and their roles in the chart.
- It supports generalization to downstream tasks
such as chart-based question answering, retrieval,
and summarization.
We hypothesize and demonstrate that explicitly
modeling the structured nature of charts leads to
more informative and task-aligned representations
compared to baseline models that treat charts as
unstructured images or pure text.

Prior Literature

Chart understanding tasks require models to reason over structured visual elements, including axes,
legends, and data marks, which general-purpose
MLLMs like GPT-4V often struggle with due to
weak visual-semantic grounding and poor layout
sensitivity (Li et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024).
Recent specialized models address these limitations. UniChart (Masry et al., 2023) introduces a
chart-focused encoder-decoder framework trained
on 611K charts, supporting tasks like summarization and chart QA. While efficient, it struggles with
compositional reasoning. ChartAssistant (Meng
et al., 2024) improves performance via a two-stage
pipeline (chart-to-table pretraining + multitask instruction tuning) and shows strong generalization
across chart types, especially in numerical and referring QA. ChartInstruct (Masry et al., 2024)
complements these with a broad instruction-tuned
dataset, enhancing chart reasoning without architectural changes.
To better encode layout-sensitive input, LayoutLMv3 (Huang et al., 2023) integrates text, vision, and layout using a unified ViT backbone. This
layout-aware modeling is better suited to structured
documents like charts, especially compared to models like MM-GEM (Ma et al., 2024), which lack
fine-grained spatial grounding.
Our approach builds on these insights by modeling chart elements as structured tokens, enriched
with class, text, and layout embeddings. We evaluate on the ChartAssistant dataset (FanqingM,
2024), which offers realistic chart-QA pairs, supporting fine-grained benchmarking of reasoning
over structured visual input.

Data

To support the training of our chart element detection model, we developed a Python-based chart
generation pipeline capable of producing synthetic
chart images (including bar, line, pie, and stacked
bar charts) with detailed annotations in the COCO
format. The pipeline uses libraries such as Matplotlib, NumPy, and Plotly to programmatically
generate diverse chart types along with structured
annotations for each visual element.
The system defines a comprehensive taxonomy
of chart components (e.g., title, axes, axis titles, tick
labels, gridlines, legends, bars, lines, and pie slices)
and extracts precise bounding boxes for each element. These annotations follow the COCO dataset

structure, capturing metadata such as bounding box
coordinates, area, category IDs, and image associations.
For each chart type, the pipeline computes spatial annotations using geometric primitives or text
layout analysis, ensuring pixel-accurate labels. It
supports various configurations, such as line style
(e.g., linear, sine, noisy), bar orientation (horizontal or vertical), and pie slice randomization. The
resulting dataset includes both the rendered chart
images and a corresponding JSON file of annotations, enabling downstream tasks such as chart
element detection and visual data extraction.
This synthetic dataset complements our manually annotated and publicly sourced data (e.g.,
ChartAssistant), providing a rich and customizable
training resource for layout-aware chart understanding.

Model

We design a Transformer-based encoder model to
learn structured visual-textual representations from
chart annotations. Each chart is represented as a
variable-length sequence of elements (e.g., bars,
titles, axes), where each element is encoded into a
fixed-size embedding using a combination of class,
bounding box, and area features.
Embedding Layer
Each chart element is represented by:
• A one-hot encoded class vector (e.g., bar, title,
x-axis, y-axis)
• A normalized bounding box: (x, y, w, h)
• A normalized area scalar
We use separate Multi-Layer Perceptrons
(MLPs) to project each of these features into a
shared embedding space of dimension D = 256,
and concatenate the resulting vectors to form the
final input embedding of dimension 3D = 768 per
token.
Transformer Encoder
The chart-level sequence of embedded elements is
fed into a Transformer encoder consisting of:
• Multi-Head Self Attention (MHSA)
• Feedforward layers with ReLU activation
• LayerNorm and residual connections

• Positional encoding via order-preserving
padding
Unlike traditional autoregressive settings, our
self-attention blocks do not use causal masks, as
the model observes all elements simultaneously.

Methods

We propose a Chart Transformer, a layout-aware,
Transformer-based architecture designed for structured understanding of chart images. The model
operates over pre-extracted chart elements—such
as bars, titles, axes, and legends—represented using
COCO-style annotations. Each element is embedded using its class label, normalized bounding box
coordinates, and area. These features are projected
into a unified embedding space, enriched with positional encodings, and passed through a Transformer
encoder. The self-attention mechanism enables the
model to reason over spatial and semantic relationships between chart elements. Chart Transformer
supports multiple downstream tasks, including element counting, chart-to-JSON conversion, and
semantic type classification.

Analysis

The Chart Transformer outperformed the Vision
Transformer (ViT), highlighting the benefits of
incorporating layout-aware reasoning through explicit element-level embeddings. While the ViT
model is effective for generic vision tasks, it struggled to capture the spatial and semantic relationships between chart components. This limitation
led to lower accuracy on tasks that required structural understanding of charts.
In contrast, the Chart Transformer—by leveraging structured element representations and positional encodings—was better suited to model interelement dependencies. This architectural advantage contributed to improved performance in the
classification task, demonstrating the importance
of layout sensitivity in chart understanding models.

Conclusion

The Chart Transformer demonstrated superior
performance compared to the Vision Transformer
(ViT) in the task of predicting the number of chart
elements, achieving an accuracy of 92%. By utilizing layout-aware embeddings and a custom architecture designed specifically for structured chart
data, the Chart Transformer was able to more effectively capture and classify chart elements than the
ViT, which lacks such specialized handling.
These results highlight the importance of
domain-specific architectures for tasks involving
complex visual structures such as charts. The performance of the Chart Transformer reinforces the
idea that incorporating knowledge of layout and
inter-element relationships can significantly enhance model accuracy in chart understanding tasks.
Future Scope
Building on the success of the Chart Transformer,
future work will explore integrating it with existing text encoder models and the Vision Transformer (ViT) to build a unified system. The aim
is to enable retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)
pipelines that can jointly process chart visuals and
textual descriptions. Such a system would support
natural language queries for retrieving and interpreting chart data, advancing the applicability of
chart understanding in real-world information retrieval and analytics settings.

Known Project Limitations

Our work is subject to several limitations that may
impact its generalization and real-world applicability.
First, our training data is largely synthetic and
may not reflect the full visual diversity of realworld charts, leading to possible performance drops
in more complex or noisy settings. Second, the annotation schema prioritizes structural elements but
may miss finer visual or stylistic details.
Additionally, our task framing simplifies chart
understanding to classification and counting, which
does not capture the full spectrum of reasoning often required in practical BI applications. Evaluation is limited to English-language datasets and
primarily Western-style chart formats, which may
introduce cultural or linguistic biases.
Users applying this system in diverse domains
or languages should account for these constraints,

and future work will aim to improve generalization,
reasoning depth, and multilingual support.
